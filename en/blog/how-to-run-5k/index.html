How to run a 5K from scratch in 8 weeks

A realistic 8-week plan to go from sedentary to finishing your first 5 km running, with safe progression and no overreach.

Start by walking — and don't be embarrassed about it

Almost everyone who runs 5 km today started by alternating running and walking. The method has a technical name (low-intensity interval running) and is the safest way to build aerobic base without overloading tendons, ligaments and joints that aren't yet adapted to impact. The heart and lungs adapt in weeks; bones and tendons take months. The alternation protects exactly the part that takes longest.

An honest start for the first week: 5 minutes of walking, then 6 blocks of 1 minute of easy running with 2 minutes of walking, and 5 minutes of walking to finish. That's 28 minutes in total. Three times a week, on non-consecutive days — Monday, Wednesday and Friday work well because they leave a recovery day between each stimulus.

The conversational-pace rule

In the first months, every run should be at conversational pace: you need to be able to say a full sentence without gasping. If you can't, you're going too fast — and running too fast too soon is the number one cause of quitting in the first weeks. The body hurts, training becomes suffering, and motivation disappears.

For most people starting out, that means running between 7:30 and 9:00 per kilometer. It seems slow, and that's the point. The gain comes from consistency, not intensity: three easy runs a week for eight weeks are worth far more than two exhausting ones that leave you sore for three days.

The 8 weeks, week by week

Weeks 1 and 2: blocks of 1 minute running and 2 walking, 6 to 8 reps. Weeks 3 and 4: 2 minutes running and 1 walking, 7 to 8 reps. Weeks 5 and 6: 4 minutes running and 1 walking, 5 reps; in week six, one of the runs becomes 10 continuous minutes. Week 7: two runs of 15 to 20 continuous minutes and one of 25. Week 8: one 20-minute run, one easy 15, and on the weekend, the 5 km — or 30 minutes, whichever comes first.

If a week gets hard, repeat it instead of moving on. That's not a delay, it's the plan working: it adjusts its pace to your adaptation, not the other way around. The order matters more than the date.

Safe weekly progression

The most used rule of thumb is to not increase weekly volume by more than 10% from one week to the next — and for beginners, something between 5% and 8% is even more prudent. Volume is the sum of minutes or kilometers across every run in the week, not the size of a single run.

Every three or four weeks, cut volume by about 30%. That down week is when the body actually absorbs the previous training. Skipping that step is what turns progress into injury: shin splints, knee pain and plantar fasciitis almost always show up after a block of weeks without a break.

Shoes, food and sleep — the minimum that matters

You don't need the most expensive shoe, but you do need a cushioned running shoe in your size (half a size up from your everyday shoes is usually right, because feet swell when running). Eat something light one to two hours before training, drink water through the day and don't run fasted while you're still adapting.

Sleep is training. It's during sleep that the body repairs tissue and consolidates adaptation. Seven hours a night is a reasonable minimum for someone starting to run; sleeping badly for several days in a row is one of the signals a good plan uses to reduce load.
