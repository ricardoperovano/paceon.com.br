What interval training is and how to do it

Hard stretches alternated with recovery: how intervals work, examples by goal and the mistakes that cancel out the workout.

A simple definition

Interval training is alternating stretches at a hard pace with stretches of recovery (jogging or walking). The idea is to accumulate more time at high intensity than would be possible continuously: running 5 × 1,000 m hard with a 2-minute jog between them is doable; running 5 km at the same pace without stopping is not.

Each session is defined by four variables: distance or time of each hard stretch, pace of the hard stretch, length and form of recovery, and number of repetitions. Changing any of them changes the stimulus — which is why "intervals" isn't one workout, but a family.

Why it works

At high intensities the body is forced to improve oxygen uptake and use (VO2max), tolerance to lactate accumulation and running economy — the energy spent to hold a pace. These are adaptations easy running alone doesn't produce, no matter how much volume you do.

Intervals also teach the brain to sustain discomfort for defined stretches, which makes a direct difference in racing: anyone who has run 6 × 800 m hard knows kilometer eight of a 10K is bearable.

Examples by goal

For a 5K: 8 × 400 m at 5K pace or slightly faster, with a 200 m jog. For a 10K: 5 or 6 × 1,000 m at 10K pace, with a 2-to-3-minute jog. For a half marathon: 4 × 2,000 m between 10K and half pace, with a 3-minute jog. For someone new to intensity: 6 × 1 minute hard with 2 minutes easy, inside a 30-minute run.

Always with a 10-to-15-minute jog warm-up (plus two or three strides) and a 10-minute cool-down. The warm-up isn't optional: it's what separates a productive workout from a muscle strain.

The mistakes that cancel out the workout

Running the first rep too fast. Intervals should be "controlled": the last reps at the same pace as the first, or even slightly faster. If you go from 4:00 to 4:40 per kilometer over the session, you went out too hard and trained fatigue, not speed.

Doing more than one a week as an amateur, or doing intervals the day after a long run. One hard workout a week (two, at most, in peak phase) is enough; the rest of the volume is easy. Intervals are the seasoning, not the meal.
