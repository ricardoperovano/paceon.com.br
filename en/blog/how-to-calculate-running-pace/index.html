How to calculate running pace (with examples)

Pace is time per kilometer. Learn to calculate it, convert to speed, predict race times and use our free calculator.

The formula

Pace is the time it takes you to cover one kilometer. The math is total time divided by distance: 30 minutes for 5 km gives 6:00 per kilometer (30 ÷ 5 = 6). If the result has decimals, convert them to seconds: 50 minutes for 8 km gives 6.25 min/km, and 0.25 minute is 15 seconds — a pace of 6:15/km.

The reverse also works: at 5:30/km, 10 km takes 55 minutes (5.5 × 10). And to know what pace you need to hold for a goal, divide the time by the distance: a 2-hour half marathon (120 minutes) needs 120 ÷ 21.1 = 5.69 min/km, or 5:41/km.

Pace, speed and the most used conversions

Speed in km/h is 60 divided by the pace in minutes: 6:00/km equals 10 km/h; 5:00/km is 12 km/h; 4:30/km is 13.3 km/h. It's useful on the treadmill, which usually shows km/h. To convert from km/h to pace, divide 60 by the speed: 11 km/h gives 5.45 min/km, or 5:27/km.

Useful references: 5 km in 25 minutes is 5:00/km; 10 km in 1 hour is 6:00/km; a half marathon in 1:45 is 4:59/km; a marathon in 4 hours is 5:41/km.

Predicting your time at another distance

Pace drops as distance grows. A well-tested rule (the Riegel formula) says the time at a new distance is the known time multiplied by (new distance ÷ known distance) to the power of 1.06. In practice: someone who runs 5 km in 25 minutes tends to run 10 km in about 52 minutes, a half marathon in 1:55 and a marathon in around 4 hours.

That prediction assumes adequate training for the new distance — a 5K runner with no long runs won't convert the time into a good marathon. Use it as a reference for setting goals, not as a promise.

Average pace, current pace and splits

The watch shows three different things. Current pace is the rhythm at this exact second, and it fluctuates a lot — don't run staring at it. Average pace is total time divided by distance so far, and it's the number that matters in a race. Splits are the pace for each kilometer, and they reveal how you distributed the effort: splits that speed up at the end (a negative split) are the pattern of the best races.
